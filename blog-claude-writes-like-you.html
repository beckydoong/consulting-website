How to Make Claude Write Exactly Like You (Not Like AI)

Seedscale Agency July 2026 9 min read

You can spot AI writing in about two seconds. The em dashes in every sentence. The "it's not just a tool, it's a partner." The relentless, weightless enthusiasm. The tidy little summary at the end that repeats everything you just read.

Your readers can spot it too. And the moment they do, they stop trusting the words, because the words clearly didn't come from you. That's the real cost of default AI writing. Not that it's bad, exactly, but that it's generic, and generic is invisible.

The fix isn't a cleverer one-off prompt you retype every time. It's a Claude Project that permanently knows two things: how you sound, and how AI sounds, so it can do the first and refuse the second. You build it once, and every draft after that comes out in your voice.

This guide walks through the whole setup. It takes about twenty minutes, and you never have to explain your voice to a blank chat window again.

What You'll End Up With

  • A reusable Claude Project that writes in your voice by default, for emails, posts, newsletters, anything
  • An anti-AI style guide that bans the words and habits that scream "a robot wrote this"
  • A voice profile Claude builds by interviewing you, so it captures how you actually talk
  • Custom instructions that make Claude self-edit before it ever shows you a draft

What You Need Before You Start

  1. A Claude account (Projects are a Pro feature, so you'll want Claude Pro)
  2. About 20 minutes, and honest answers about how you write
  3. Optional but powerful: two or three samples of your best real writing

You don't need any technical skill. If you can paste text into a box and answer questions about yourself, you can do this.

First, Why AI Writing Sounds Like AI

Before you fix it, it helps to know exactly what you're fixing. These are the tells your readers register, even when they can't name them:

The tell What it looks like
Filler openers "In today's fast-paced world," "In the ever-evolving landscape of…"
The fake-profound flip "It's not just X. It's Y."
Corporate verbs leverage, unlock, elevate, empower, supercharge, delve, unleash
Em-dash addiction A dash in every other sentence, doing a comma's job
The bow-tie ending A closing paragraph that summarizes everything you just said
Uniform rhythm Every sentence the same medium length, like a metronome

Your style guide's job is to name these directly so Claude has a checklist to avoid. Your voice profile's job is to give it something to reach for instead. You need both.

Step-by-Step Setup

1 Create the Claude Project (2 minutes)

A Project is a workspace that keeps its own instructions and knowledge files, so Claude remembers your setup across every chat inside it.

  1. Go to claude.ai and sign in
  2. In the left sidebar, click Projects, then Create Project
  3. Name it something like "Write in My Voice" and create it

Everything in the next steps lives inside this Project. Start every writing chat from here and the setup applies automatically.

2 Add Your Anti-AI Style Guide (5 minutes)

This is a document that tells Claude what not to do. Copy the starter guide below, tweak anything that doesn't fit you, and add it to your Project's knowledge (look for Add content or the paperclip, and paste it as a file named style-guide.md).

# Anti-AI Writing Style Guide Rules for writing that doesn't sound like a robot wrote it. Follow these on everything. ## Banned words and phrases Never use: leverage (as a verb), unlock, unleash, elevate, empower, supercharge, delve, seamless, robust, game-changer, tapestry, beacon, testament to, navigate the complexities, realm, foster, embark. Never use these openers: "In today's fast-paced world," "In the ever-evolving landscape," "Whether you're a [X] or a [Y]," "Ever wondered…?" Never use the flip: "It's not just [X], it's [Y]." Never use hype transitions: "Let's dive in," "buckle up," "here's the kicker," "the best part?" ## Punctuation and formatting Stop using em dashes as a default connector. One every few paragraphs at most. Prefer a period or a comma. No emoji unless I ask for them. Don't bold a phrase for emphasis in every paragraph. Let the words carry it. Vary sentence length on purpose. Short ones. Then a longer one with a real clause inside it. Robots make every sentence the same length. ## Structure Get to the point in the first sentence. No throat-clearing, no "picture this," no dictionary definitions. One idea per paragraph. Blunt is fine. Do not end with a summary paragraph that repeats the piece. End on the sharpest line. Cut hedging: arguably, it's worth noting, in many ways, quite, very, really, just. ## Voice Write like a smart person talking to another smart person, not a brand talking to a market. Use concrete nouns and real examples instead of abstractions. Contractions are good. Sentence fragments are fine. If a sentence could sit in anyone's blog, rewrite it so it could only be mine.
Make it yours. The banned list above is a starting point. Every time you catch a word you personally never say, add it. A style guide with ten words you actually hate beats a generic one with fifty.

3 Have Claude Interview You to Build Your Voice Profile (10 minutes)

This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that makes the whole thing work. Instead of trying to describe your own voice from a blank page (hard, and you'll get it wrong), you let Claude pull it out of you with an interview.

Open a new chat inside your Project and paste this prompt:

You're going to help me build a writing voice profile so you can write as me from now on. Interview me one question at a time. Ask a single question, wait for my answer, then ask the next. Do not move on until an answer is clear, and dig deeper when I'm vague or generic. Cover, at minimum: - Who I am and what I do - Who I'm writing for, and the reaction I want them to have - Words and phrases I genuinely say - Words and phrases I would never use - How formal or casual I am, and where that changes - My humor, if any, and how dry or warm it is - My sentence rhythm, and how I feel about rules like "never start with 'And'" - Writers, creators, or brands whose voice I admire, and why Ask at least 10 questions. When you have enough to capture how I actually sound, stop interviewing and output ONE Markdown document titled "My Voice Profile" with these sections: 1. Who I Am 2. Who I'm Writing For 3. My Voice in 5 Rules 4. Words & Phrases I Use 5. Words & Phrases to Avoid 6. Punctuation & Formatting Habits 7. Do / Never 8. Three sample sentences written in my voice Write it so another AI could read it cold and sound exactly like me. Start with your first question now.

Answer honestly, in your normal typing voice (don't clean it up, that is the data). When Claude produces the "My Voice Profile" document, read it, fix anything that's off, then save it to your Project knowledge as voice-profile.md.

Why the interview beats a form. You know your voice the way you know your own accent: you can't hear it, but everyone else can. The questions surface things you'd never think to write down, and your unedited answers are the truest sample of how you sound.

4 Set the Project's Custom Instructions (2 minutes)

Now connect the two files with a standing order. In your Project, find Set custom instructions (or "What should Claude know") and paste this:

Write everything in my voice, following "voice-profile.md" and "style-guide.md" in this project's knowledge. Sound like me, not like AI. Before you show me any draft, silently check it against the style guide: remove banned words, kill extra em dashes, vary the sentence lengths, and cut the summary ending. Show me the corrected version, not your first attempt. If you're unsure how I'd phrase something, ask me instead of guessing in a generic voice.

That instruction does the quiet work: Claude drafts, self-edits against your rules, and only then hands you the result. You get the second draft without asking for it.

Other Recommendations That Make a Real Difference

The three files above are enough to start. These extras are what separate "pretty good" from "people ask if you wrote it yourself."

  • Feed it your greatest hits. Add two or three samples of writing you're proud of to the Project knowledge, in a file called voice-samples.md. Label it: "These are examples of my real voice. Match this." Real samples teach more than any description.
  • Always ask for the second draft, out loud, too. Even with the self-edit instruction, saying "now give me a sharper version that sounds more like me" almost always improves it. The first draft is the warm-up.
  • Correct it in the moment, then update the file. When Claude slips a "leverage" back in, don't just fix that draft. Add "leverage" to your banned list. The Project gets smarter every time you catch it.
  • Give it the job, not just the topic. "Write a LinkedIn post" is vague. "Write a LinkedIn post for founders who feel behind on AI, in my voice, 150 words, one idea, no hashtags" gets you something usable on the first pass.
  • Keep one Project, not ten. A single well-tuned "Write in My Voice" Project you refine over months beats a new chat every time. The compounding is the point.

How to Actually Use It Day to Day

Once it's set up, the workflow is simple. Start a new chat inside the Project and tell it what you need in plain English:

  • "Turn these three bullet points into a newsletter intro in my voice."
  • "Rewrite this paragraph so it sounds like me and loses the AI tells."
  • "Draft a reply to this email. Warm, short, my voice." (paste the email)
  • "Here's my rough voice memo transcript. Clean it into a post without flattening how I talk."

The test of whether it's working: paste something Claude wrote into a group chat with people who know you, without saying it was AI. If nobody blinks, you're done.

Troubleshooting

"It still slips into AI voice sometimes." Two fixes. Make sure both files are actually attached to the Project (not pasted into one old chat), and add the specific offending word or phrase to your style guide the moment you see it. The guide should grow.

"The voice profile feels generic." Your interview answers were probably too clean. Redo Step 3 and answer like you're texting a friend, not filling out a form. Let it be messy. That mess is your voice.

"I don't have Claude Pro / can't use Projects." You can still do a lighter version: paste the style guide and voice profile at the top of each new chat. It works, you just have to re-paste. The Project simply makes it permanent.

"How often should I update it?" Whenever you catch a miss, and every few months as your voice evolves. Five minutes of upkeep keeps it sharp.

The Bottom Line

AI doesn't sound like AI because it's incapable of sounding like you. It sounds like AI because nobody told it not to, and nobody showed it who you are. Spend twenty minutes fixing both, once, and you get back every hour you'd have spent rewriting robotic drafts, without ever publishing something that sounds like everyone else.
